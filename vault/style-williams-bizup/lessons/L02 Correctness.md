# L02 Correctness

**Summary.** Correctness looks like obedience but involves choice. Grammar rules have social power (Standard English arose from geography and economic power, not logic; it is often less logical than nonstandard forms) but "errors" do not signal poor minds. Three kinds of rule: Real (define English), Social (Standard vs. nonstandard), Invented. Only invented rules trouble educated writers; they split into Folklore (ignore), Elegant Options (formal flavor), and Hobgoblins. A few word-usage distinctions still attract notice. Pronoun agreement and gender bias are an open problem with only imperfect options. The lesson sits first so correctness can be put "behind us" and attention go to clarity.

## Principles
- [[Put correctness behind you and spend effort on clarity]] - precision is not just grammar.
- [[Observe real and social rules]] - agreement, articles, Standard forms: just follow.
- [[Do not enforce folklore rules]] - leave and/but, that/which, less/fewer, since/while alone.
- [[Begin sentences with and, but, or because]] - plus how to place *because* clauses.
- [[Choose that or which by sound]] - Fowler's rule is not usage; may adopt for softness.
- [[Treat elegant options as tone choices]] - split infinitives, whom, final prepositions, none.
- [[Use who and whom by the grammar of their own clause]] - with the delete test.
- [[Make pronouns agree with their antecedents]] - incl. collective nouns.
- [[Handle generic pronouns without bias]] - options and costs.
- [[Learn the word distinctions careful readers notice]] - and avoid irregardless.

## Summing up (condensed)
- Write correctly, but separate fact from folklore, or you miss what matters: choices that make prose dense/wordy vs. clear/concise.
- Grammatically perfect prose can still be unreadable (example: "Too precise a specification of information processing requirements incurs the risk of overestimation ...").
- Rule-obsessed writers often assert a personal style (fastidious "class"); fine if it is not a pretext to discriminate and stays subordinate to clarity and grace.

## Notable quotes
> "You have to learn the rules one-by-one and accept the fact that some of them, probably most of them, are arbitrary and idiosyncratic."
> "If writers whom we judge to be competent regularly violate some alleged rule and most careful readers never notice, then the rule has no force."
