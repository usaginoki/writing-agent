# L07 Motivation

**Summary:** Part Three turns from sentences to larger units. Most readers are neither deeply engaged nor expert, so introductions must motivate them (a problem they care about, not a topic) and tell them what to expect. The default introduction: [prelude] + shared context + problem (condition + cost) + solution/main point. Problems are practical (action) or conceptual (understanding). Conclusions can mirror the introduction in reverse.

## Principles
- [[Pose a problem, not just a topic]]
- [[Open with a shared context you can qualify]]
- [[Signal the problem with but or however]]
- [[State a problem as condition plus cost]]
- [[Find the cost by asking So what]]
- [[Choose practical or conceptual framing deliberately]]
- [[State practical costs that matter to your readers]]
- [[Frame conceptual problems as ignorance plus a larger question]]
- [[End the introduction with a solution that names key themes]]
- [[Use a prelude only when the genre allows]]
- [[Write conclusions by reversing the introduction]]
- [[State a problem even for an assigned topic]]

## Summing up (condensed)
State a problem readers want solved. Practical: costs clear enough that readers ask "What do we do?"; sequence shared context > *but* condition > cost > action. Conceptual: a small question worth answering because it helps answer a larger one; sequence shared context > *but we don't know* > larger question > answer.

## Quotes
> "A problem well-put is half solved." (Dewey)
> "...a conceptual problem... demands a change in understanding as a solution."
