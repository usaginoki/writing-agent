# Do not inflate prose to seem deep

**Rule:** Never add abstraction, nominal phrases, or length to look profound or to disguise thin knowledge. Say it plainly.
**Why (reader effect):** Readers cannot tell dense writing from deep thinking and may blame themselves; the "style of exclusion" (bureaucratese, legalese, academese) hides ideas even from the writer and is passed on by imitation.

## Diagnose
- Long sentences full of abstract nouns ("causal factors", "recognition of the fact").
- Verbs turned into phrases: noun/adjective + general verb (prove, serve, form, play, render).
- Passive wherever possible; noun constructions instead of gerunds ("by examination of" for "by examining").
- You write this way when unsure of your subject: a red flag about your understanding, not just style.

## Revise
1. Restate the point in simple words; if you cannot, clarify your thinking first.
2. Replace verb phrases with single verbs; passive with active; nominal phrases with gerunds/clauses.
3. Cut by roughly half where possible without losing content.

## Examples
> ✗ Pretentious writers avoid simple verbs. ... (Orwell's own sentence on this is itself nominal; see [[Patterns - Turgid to plain]].)
> ✓ Pretentious writers avoid simple verbs. Instead of using one word, such as break, stop, kill, they turn the verb into a noun or adjective, then tack onto it a general-purpose verb.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Some complexity is necessary in the material; only gratuitous complexity in the writing is the target (see [[Use the principles to mentally revise difficult reading]]).
