# Use I and we with metadiscourse verbs

**Rule:** In academic and scientific prose, use active first person (`I/we`) with verbs naming the writer's own thinking and writing (metadiscourse: *argue, show, claim, suggest, cite, define, summarize*). Use the passive for research actions anyone could perform (*measure, record, observe, add, use*).
**Why (reader effect):** The claim that scholars must avoid `I/we` for objectivity is false: journals like *Science* use `We examine... We compare...` regularly. Deleting `I` does not make thinking more objective; people still do the thinking.

## Diagnose
- Passive verb naming the writer's act of arguing/defining/showing (`It is argued`, `The terms must be defined`)? Make it active.
- Active first-person verb narrating a routine lab/field action (`I added preparations`)? Consider passive.
- Excess: sentences opening `I think/I believe`, or a diary of your thinking (`First I read... Then I considered`).

## Revise
1. Classify the verb: **metadiscourse** (only the writer can do it) vs **research procedure** (anyone can).
2. Metadiscourse -> `we/I` + active; procedure -> passive (`preparations were added`).
3. Check for dangling modifiers created by the passive (below).

## Examples
> ✗ The terms of the analysis must be defined.
> ✓ We must define the terms of the analysis.

> ✓ To determine if monokines elicited a response, preparations were added. (conventional passive for procedure)

## When not to apply / exceptions
- The procedure passive can dangle its introductory modifier (implied `I/we` vs explicit `preparations`); scientific communities accept it as standard.
- Some teachers ban `I` to stop `I think/I believe` padding and narrative of one's thinking; follow them there.
- Metadiscourse appears mostly in introductions (announce) and conclusions (summarize); see [[Metadiscourse]]. The impersonal "scientific" style is modern: Newton wrote in first person (1672).
