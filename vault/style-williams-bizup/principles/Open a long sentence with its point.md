# Open a long sentence with its point

**Rule:** In a long sentence, state the key claim first in a short, simple main clause with a short concrete subject; then add the longer, more complex explanation or support. The same simple-before-complex logic applies to paragraphs, sections, and documents. (p. 149)
**Why (reader effect):** A point stated up front frames the complexity that follows, so readers anticipate the relevance of later words "even before we read them". A delayed or dribbled point forces readers to reconstruct it and reassemble the sentence; the sentence "feels backward".

## Diagnose
- Find the sentence's [[Point]] (key claim). If it sits in the middle or end, after 20+ words of support, revise ([[Point-first test]]).
- Is the subject long and abstract while the concrete character appears late?

## Revise
1. Make the point a short main clause (e.g. 8 words) with a short concrete subject and specific action verb.
2. Append the support/explanation after it (often a *when* clause).
3. Scale up: open a paragraph with its point (pp. 119-120), a section with a point paragraph (pp. 114-115), a document with an introduction stating its point (pp. 99-108).

## Examples
> ✗ High-deductible health plans and Health Saving Accounts into which workers and their employers make tax-deductible deposits result in workers taking more responsibility for their health care. [support … point]
> ✓ Workers take more responsibility for their health care when they adopt high-deductible insurance plans and Health Saving Accounts into which they and their employers make tax-deductible deposits. [point … support]
> ✗ A new sales initiative that has created a close integration between the garden and home products departments has made significant improvements to the customer services that Acme offers.
> ✓ Acme has significantly improved its customer services with a new sales initiative that closely integrates the garden and home products departments.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Competing principle: the periodic sentence deliberately delays the main clause for impact (pp. 145-146); see [[Get to the subject of the main clause quickly]].
