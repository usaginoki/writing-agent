# Use a prelude only when the genre allows

**Rule:** If you want an attention-catching opening, use a prelude (quotation, startling fact, illustrative anecdote) that vividly introduces themes central to your problem, placed before the shared context. What really catches attention is a problem in need of a solution.
**Why (reader effect):** a prelude makes the theme concrete but does not itself motivate; it supplements the problem.

## Diagnose
- Does the opening hook relate to key themes of the problem? Or is it a gimmick?
- Is the genre appropriate? Rare in natural/social sciences, more common in humanities, most common in general-audience writing.

## Revise
1. Pick quotation, fact, or anecdote tied to your themes (can combine all three).
2. Follow it with shared context, then problem, then solution.
3. Optionally echo it in a coda ([[Patterns - Conclusions]]).

## Examples
> ✓ prelude: "'If you're old enough to fight for your country, you're old enough to drink to it.'... Jim S., president of Omega Alpha, no longer has a chance to do either."

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Skip in scientific writing; never use it in place of a stated problem.
