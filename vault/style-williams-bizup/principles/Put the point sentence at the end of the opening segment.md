# Put the point sentence at the end of the opening segment

**Rule:** Make the point sentence the last sentence of the opening segment: the last thing readers read before the longer, more complex segment. One-sentence opening: it is the point by default. Two-sentence: the point is the second. For long section/document introductions, still put it at the end.
**Why (reader effect):** points buried at the end of a unit make it seem to ramble until readers retrospectively make sense of it, costing extra effort. Document-wide, point sentences form the outline; missing them reads as incoherent.

## Diagnose
- Underline the sentence that states the unit's point. Where is it: first? last? absent?
- Is it directly before the long body?

## Revise
1. Move the point from the end into the opening segment's final sentence.
2. Drop/merge weak preliminary sentences.
3. Restate it at the end of the unit as a conclusion if useful ("We thus conclude...").

## Examples
> ✗ opens: "Thirty sixth-grade students wrote essays that were analyzed to determine the effectiveness of eight weeks of training..." (point only at the end)
> ✓ "In this study, thirty sixth-grade students were taught to distinguish fact from opinion. They did so successfully during the instruction period, but the effect was inconsistent and less than predicted, and six months after instruction ended, the instruction had no measurable effect."

## When not to apply / exceptions
- If you have good reason to save the main point for the conclusion, see [[Promise a withheld point at the end of the introduction]].
- Some paragraphs make their point at the end and still work when the section's point is clear ([[Introduce sections clearly so imperfect paragraphs survive]]).
- Fear that stating the point bores readers is unfounded if they are motivated by a problem.
