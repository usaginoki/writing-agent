# End the introduction with a solution that names key themes

**Rule:** Close the introduction with the solution/main point/claim, in the stress position, stating the key themes the rest will develop.
**Why (reader effect):** readers look there for the point; naming themes lets them organize the body. Telling the point does not bore readers who are motivated by a problem.

## Diagnose
- Underline the solution. Is it at the end of the introduction?
- Practical: does it state an action? Conceptual: does it state what readers should understand/believe?
- Do its key terms recur in the body? ([[Key-term recurrence test]])

## Revise
1. Move the point sentence to the end of the introduction.
2. Add key theme words (see [[Name key themes toward the end of the point sentence]]).
3. If the point must wait, see [[Promise a withheld point at the end of the introduction]].

## Examples
> practical: "We may not be able to stop bingeing entirely, but we must try to control its worst costs by educating students in how to manage its risks."
> conceptual: "This study reports on our analysis of the beliefs of 300 first-year students. We found..."

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Withholding the point for the conclusion is allowed with good reason (see linked principle).
