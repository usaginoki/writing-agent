# Use a comma after a long introductory element and skip it after a short one

**Rule:** After a long (four or five words or more) introductory phrase/clause, put a comma before the subject (needed). After a short one, the comma is optional; omitting it speeds readers up.
**Why (reader effect):** Long openers need a breath and a boundary; commas after short ones slow readers just when they should accelerate.

## Diagnose
- Count words in the opener: short (<4) vs long (4-5+).
- Check against the five reliable rules (comments on the whole sentence still take a comma).

## Revise
1. Long opener: add the comma.
2. Short opener: drop the comma unless misreading is possible.

## Examples
> ✓ Once again we find similar responses to such stimuli. / In 1945 few realized how the war had transformed us.
> ✓ When a lawyer begins her opening statement with a dry recital of the law ... before the court, the jury is likely to nod off.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- It is not wrong to put a comma after a short opener (p. 219). Misreading risk overrides.
