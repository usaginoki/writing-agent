# Choose practical or conceptual framing deliberately

**Rule:** Decide first whether you want readers to *do* something (practical problem, action solution) or *think/understand* something (conceptual problem, informational solution), and write condition, cost, and solution all in that mode.
**Why (reader effect):** each kind motivates differently; mixing modes leaves readers unsure what you are asking of them.

## Diagnose
- Ask: what should the reader do or think after reading?
- Practical: condition in the world, cost = pain/loss, solution = action ("we must...").
- Conceptual: condition = something unknown, cost = a larger unknown, solution = a finding or claim ("we found...").

## Revise
1. Classify the problem.
2. Check each part matches the type ([[Diagnose whether an introduction motivates]] step 5).
3. To test, rewrite a conceptual problem as practical and vice versa (Exercise 7.1) and note what changes.

## Examples
> practical: "...we must try to control its worst costs by educating students in how to manage its risks."
> conceptual: "We found that students were more likely to binge if they knew more stories of other students bingeing..."

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Non-academic writers mostly pose practical problems, academics mostly conceptual; follow genre.
- A conceptual problem about not knowing *how to do* something is still conceptual: its solution is information, not action.
