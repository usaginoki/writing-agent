# Do not chain subordinate clauses after the main clause

**Rule:** After the main clause, do not add one subordinate (especially relative) clause to another to another. Revise with one of four moves: cut, split, change clauses to modifying phrases, or coordinate. (pp. 151-153)
**Why (reader effect):** Even with the point first, readers must slog through a shapeless tacked-on string; each *which/that* forces them to figure out what it refers to. See [[Sprawl]].

## Diagnose
- Read aloud; hesitation, stumbling, or running out of breath signals sprawl ([[Sprawl read-aloud test]]).
- Mark each *who/which/that*; a chain of 3+ is sprawl ([[Relative pronoun trace]]).

## Revise
1. **Cut:** reduce relative clauses to phrases by deleting *who/that/which* + *is/was*; occasionally recast the verb as *-ing* (*numbers that will identify* → *numbers identifying*).
2. **Split:** turn subordinate clauses into independent sentences.
3. **Modifying phrases:** use [[Resumptive Modifier]], [[Summative Modifier]], or [[Free Modifier]] (see [[Extend sentences with resumptive, summative, and free modifiers]]).
4. **Coordinate:** see [[Extend sentences with coordination]].
5. Quick fix for the reader: replace each relative pronoun with its noun to unravel.

## Examples
> ✗ No scientific advance is more exciting than genetic engineering, which is a new way of manipulating the elemental structural units of life itself, which are the genes and chromosomes that tell our cells how to reproduce to become the parts that constitute our bodies.
> ✓ (cut) …few are more promising than genetic engineering, a new way of manipulating the elemental structural units of life itself, the genes and chromosomes that tell our cells how to reproduce to become the parts that constitute our bodies.
> ✓ (split) Many areas of science are important to our future, but few are more promising than genetic engineering. It is a new way of manipulating…
