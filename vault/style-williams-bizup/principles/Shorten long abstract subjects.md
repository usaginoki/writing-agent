# Shorten long abstract subjects

**Rule:** Keep whole subjects short (underline any longer than 7-8 words). Turn a nominalization in a long subject into a verb with its own subject; turn a long relative clause in the subject into a *when/if* clause, a trailing clause, or a separate sentence. (p. 146)
**Why (reader effect):** Readers wait through the long subject for a verb; abstract nominalized subjects hide characters and actions.

## Diagnose
- Underline whole subjects ([[Subject (Simple and Whole)]]); flag > 7-8 words ([[Long subject test]]).
- Does it contain a [[Nominalization]] or a long relative clause (*that/who…*)?

## Revise
1. Nominalization case: make it a verb, find a subject (character) for it; the old subject becomes a character.
2. Relative-clause case: convert to an introductory *when/if* clause.
3. If that clause is long too, move it to the end, provided (a) the main clause is short and states the point, and (b) the moved clause carries newer, more complex information that supports the main clause.
4. Or better, split: one sentence for the clause content, then a short sentence with a short subject ("Such companies…").

## Examples
> ✗ Abco Inc.'s understanding of the drivers of its profitability in the Asian market for small electronics helped it pursue opportunities in Africa.
> ✓ Abco Inc. was able to pursue opportunities in Africa because it understood what drove profitability in the Asian market for small electronics.
> ✗ A company that focuses on hiring the best personnel and then trains them … is likely to earn the loyalty of its employees.
> ✓ When a company focuses on hiring … , it is likely to earn the loyalty of its employees. / A company is likely to earn the loyalty of its employees when it focuses on hiring … / Some companies focus on hiring … . Such companies are likely to earn the loyalty of their employees.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- None stated in source (the 7-8 word figure is a rule of thumb).
