# Join sentences with a comma and a coordinating conjunction

**Rule:** Comma + *and, but, yet, for, so, or, nor* + new subject and verb is a conventional sentence boundary. Choice (not rule) among period, semicolon, or comma + conjunction.
**Why (reader effect):** Readers recognize the boundary from the two signals. Too many *and/so* joins feel simplistic; avoid more than one or two per page (p. 212).

## Diagnose
- Look at each ", and/but/so" : is a subject + verb following? If yes it is a sentence join; if no it is a compound predicate (no comma needed, see [[Do not put a comma between two coordinated elements]]).
- Three or more joined sentences: do any have internal punctuation?

## Revise
1. Long sentences with internal punctuation: use a period instead.
2. Series of three+ short sentences: commas only if each is short and unpunctuated; otherwise semicolons.
3. Period + *But* if what follows is important and will be developed; comma + *but* if it only qualifies (p. 213-214).
4. Optional comma omission for a short pair after a shared introductory modifier (see below).

## Examples
> ✓ American intellectuals have often followed Europeans, but our culture has proven inhospitable to their brand of socialism.
> ✓ Baseball satisfies our admiration for precision, basketball speaks to our love of speed and grace, and football appeals to our lust for violence.
> ✓ Once the upheaval ... had settled down, the economies of its former satellites had begun to rebound but Russia's had yet to hit bottom. (no comma: shared modifier)

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Less common ends: period + conjunction ("Education cannot guarantee a democracy. And when it is...", ok, once or twice a page); semicolon + conjunction; conjunction alone (best writers do it, some teachers call it an error); comma alone for very short linked clauses ("Act in haste, repent at leisure"; many teachers disapprove).
