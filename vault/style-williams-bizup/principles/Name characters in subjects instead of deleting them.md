# Name characters in subjects instead of deleting them

**Rule:** Do not drop all characters from a sentence (`A decision was made in favor of doing a study`). Supply a doer; if the doer is "people in general", choose among `researchers/social critics/taxpayers`, `we`, `one`, or `you`.
**Why (reader effect):** Readers have the most trouble with sentences lacking all characters: "Who fears? Who recommends?" Context may help but invites wrong guesses.

## Diagnose
- Sentence has no person/group; passive with no `by`-phrase; `There was...` openings.
- Ask: could the sentence mean two different who-does-what readings?

## Revise
1. Decide the real doer(s); name them in the subject.
2. For generic doers: a general noun (`researchers`) is best. `one` feels stiff; `we` may be ambiguous (writer only? writer+others? reader+writer? everyone?). `you` is inappropriate unless you address the reader directly.
3. Beware: avoiding both nominalizations and vague pronouns tends to push writers into passives.
4. Quick Tip: when explaining a complicated matter to someone involved, imagine them across the table and use `you`; if `you` doesn't fit, switch to a fitting group (`taxpayers`).

## Examples
> ✗ A decision was made in favor of doing a study of the disagreements.
> ✓ We decided that I should study why they disagreed. (one of several possible readings)

> ✗ Taxable intangible property includes financial notes... A one-time tax of 2% on its value applies to this property.
> ✓ You have to pay tax on your intangible property, including your financial notes... On this property, you pay a one-time tax of 2%.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Deliberate impersonality or misdirection (Lesson 12; the chancellor-letter exercise 4.5).
- English has "no good solution" for a generic doer (p. 52).
