# Get to the subject of the main clause quickly

**Rule:** Do not open sentences with long introductory phrases or clauses. Reach the main subject (and verb) within the first few words; move or split a long opener. (p. 145)
**Why (reader effect):** Readers must hold the opener in memory while waiting for a main subject and verb still to come; the memory load hinders understanding.

## Diagnose
- Underline the first 7-8 words of each sentence ([[First seven words test]]); is the main subject and verb in them?
- Count words before the main verb (1a: 31 words before *should not load up*; 1b: 17 words before subject and verb).
- Opener starts with *if, since, when, although*, and is long.

## Revise
1. Move the long introductory clause to the end of the sentence.
2. If it will not fit there, make it a sentence of its own (or an independent clause joined by *so/but*).
3. If you must open with a subordinate clause, keep it short (these clauses tend to come before main clauses in English).
4. Limit long openers to a few sentences in a text.

## Examples
> ✗ Since most undergraduate students change their major fields of study at least once during their college careers, first-year students who are not certain about the program of studies they want to pursue should not load up their schedules…
> ✓ First-year students should not load up their schedules with requirements for a particular program if they are not certain about the program of studies they want to pursue, because most change their major fields of study at least once during their college careers.
> ✓ (summing up) Most undergraduate students change their major fields … at least once …, so first-year students should not load up their schedules … if they are not certain …

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Periodic (suspended) style: pile up introductory clauses deliberately to delay and heighten a closing main clause ("When a society spends more on its pets than on its homeless, when it rewards… it has lost its moral center"). Use sparingly; stress the last words (p. 146; see Lesson 11).
- Short openers (e.g. a short *if*-clause) are fine.
