# Turn nominalizations into verbs

**Rule:** When an abstract noun derived from a verb or adjective names an action, change it back to a verb (or adjective) and give it a character as subject. Nominalizations are the main marker of turgid prose, worst when they are subjects of verbs.
**Why (reader effect):** Nominalizations force extra articles and prepositions, blur who acts, and hide logical relations, so the prose feels abstract, indirect, difficult.

## Diagnose
- Scan for suffixes `-tion, -ment, -ance/-ence, -ity, -ness, -al, -ure`, gerunds (`our singing`), and zero-form nouns (`request, review, hope, result, repair`).
- Check adjectives that hide actions (`applicable` -> `applies`).
- Worst cases: nominalization as the subject of an empty verb; two or three joined by prepositions (`a review of the evolution of the brain`).

## Revise
Use the three-step routine from [[Diagnose characters and actions]]:
1. Verbify the nominalization (`intention -> intend`, `loss -> lose`).
2. Find who does it; make that character the subject.
3. Rebuild the sentence; link clauses with `because, if, when, although, why, how, whether, that`.
See [[Patterns - Nominalization to verb]] for the five recurring shapes.

## Examples
> ✗ The intention of the committee is to audit the records.
> ✓ The committee intends to audit the records.

> ✗ The agency conducted an investigation into the matter.
> ✓ The agency investigated the matter.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Keep nominalizations in four cases ([[Keep useful nominalizations]]): anaphoric short subject, replacement for `The fact that`, object-naming noun, familiar concept as virtual character.
- Verb and noun are sometimes the same word (`request`, `review`); judge by function, not form.
- Revising an abstract passage may require inventing characters ([[Name characters in subjects instead of deleting them]]).
