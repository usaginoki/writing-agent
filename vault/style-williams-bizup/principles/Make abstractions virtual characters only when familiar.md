# Make abstractions virtual characters only when familiar

**Rule:** Abstractions may serve as main characters if readers know them and you make them subjects of a series of verbs that tell a story. For an unfamiliar abstract character, tell the story from a human viewpoint and do not surround it with other nominalizations.
**Why (reader effect):** A familiar abstraction (`studies`) reads as a character; an unfamiliar one (`prospective intention`) amid other abstractions makes the passage needlessly dense.

## Diagnose
- Is the abstract subject familiar to this audience? Is it subject of several consecutive actions?
- Count nominalizations around it; many = trouble.
- Fix path: find the flesh-and-blood actors behind it (`we use intention to plan`).

## Revise
1. Keep a familiar abstraction as a consistent subject across sentences.
2. If unfamiliar, recast with human characters (`I`, `we`) and verbs: `We use prospective intention to represent how we have acted...`.
3. Keep the technical term but cut surrounding nominalizations.
4. Reader of hard text: retell story one action at a time; if that fails, list character-action pairs and rewrite.

## Examples
> ✓ To understand what causes psychiatric disorder, studies should look for more than one variable rather than adopt a strategy in which they test only one... (`studies` = virtual character)

> ✗ The cognitive function of prospective intention is the representation of a subject's similar past actions...
> ✓ We use prospective intention to represent how we have acted in our past and present and how we will act in the future.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Different form may shift meaning; a passage means what careful, competent readers take it to mean (p. 52).
- You often must write about abstractions; that is allowed.
