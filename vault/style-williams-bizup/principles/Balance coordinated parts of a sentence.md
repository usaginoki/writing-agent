# Balance coordinated parts of a sentence

**Rule:** When a point deserves a flourish, coordinate (and, or, nor, but, yet) parts that echo one another in word order, sound, structure and meaning; make each later element a bit longer than the one before.
**Why (reader effect):** Symmetry is what makes a sentence feel graceful; unbalanced parts "lurch" from one to the next. Careful readers notice and remember balanced closers.

## Diagnose
- Find a sentence whose parts are logically parallel (two clauses, paired objects, paired qualities) but are built differently.
- Mark topics of corresponding phrases and the stressed words; do they echo each other (administration / opposition; arbitrary / revolutionary / irreconcilable)?
- Check whether repeated subjects keep the same form (Lippmann keeps *citizens* as subject of each clause, even choosing a passive to preserve the balance).

## Revise
1. Line up the corresponding parts under each other.
2. Give them the same grammatical shape and matching topics.
3. Pair meanings: force/feel, affect/dominate, habit/necessity, give/take.
4. Order elements short to long; end on the heaviest.
5. Let a short sentence pair balance a short one (*balance no longer exists* / *democracy perishes* / *freedom cannot be maintained*).
6. Use a frame such as *not only . . . but also . . .*; it forces you to find the second half (and generates thought).

## Examples
> ✗ ...impracticable for an administration to be arbitrary against a revolutionary opposition that is irreconcilably opposed to it.
> ✓ ...impracticable for the administration to be arbitrary and for the opposition to be revolutionary and irreconcilable. (Lippmann)

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Overused, balance "can seem merely clever"; reserve it for important points or concluding a line of reasoning (p. 173).
- Do not distort meaning to force symmetry.
