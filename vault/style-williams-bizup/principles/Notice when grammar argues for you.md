# Notice when grammar argues for you

**Rule:** Your choice of subjects and verbs makes claims (who is free, who is coerced, who is responsible) beyond your explicit argument. Choose them knowingly, and ask whether you would accept being persuaded this way.
**Why (reader effect):** Readers absorb the implied philosophy of action without noticing it. Jefferson made King George the active subject of all abuses (Part 2), the colonists the agents of petitions (Part 3), and in Part 1 made abstractions (necessity, prudence, experience, respect) force the colonists, implying they had no choice.

## Diagnose
- Chart who or what is the subject across a passage: person, group, or abstraction?
- Do these choices imply who acts freely and who is compelled?
- Could the opposite pattern (colonists as victims, or the king as circumstance) be written?

## Revise
1. Make the pattern match what you are willing to state explicitly.
2. If the grammar carries a premise, say the premise.
3. Judge by motive and end: the book says we accept Jefferson only if his end justified the means, a principle we usually reject.

## Examples
> ✗ ...it is necessary for one people to dissolve the political bands...
> ✓ ...when we decide we must dissolve the political bands...
> ✗ Shiites and Sunnis distrust one another because they have engaged in conflict. (agents)
> ✓ Generations of cultural conflict have caused distrust among Shiites and Sunnis. (circumstance)

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Abstract subjects can be right for general principles (Part 1 was a philosophy of just revolution in general).
