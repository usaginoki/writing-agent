# Begin sentences with old information

**Rule:** Open each sentence with information familiar to readers: words from the sentence just read, or general knowledge of the subject.
**Why (reader effect):** familiar material first gives a foothold; opening with new material makes passages feel choppy and as if information "comes out of nowhere".

## Diagnose
- Underline the first words of each sentence. Did readers meet these ideas just before, or can they infer them from the subject?
- You can't reliably judge your own text (all seems old to you): have a reader underline new info ([[Old-new test]]).

## Revise
1. Identify what the previous sentence ended with.
2. Move that (or its familiar relative) to the front, as [[Topic]].
3. Move what is new to the end ([[End sentences with new information]]); use passive or other shifts if needed ([[Use the passive to put old information first]]).

## Examples
> ✗ Only the children of the wealthiest families ... will be able to afford a college education if this trend continues. Knowledge and intellectual skills, in addition to wealth, will divide us ...
> ✓ If this trend continues, a college education will soon be affordable only by the children of the wealthiest families. When that happens, we will be divided ... by knowledge and intellectual skills.

> ✗ The number of dead in the Civil War exceeded all other wars in American history combined.
> ✓ Of all the wars in American history, none has exceeded the Civil War in the number of dead.

## When not to apply / exceptions
- If it conflicts with other clarity principles, old-before-new still wins ([[Favor cohesion over single-sentence clarity]]), but it usually cooperates with characters-as-subjects ([[Character]]) (mentioned characters are old).
- Opening with new material can serve deliberate emphasis; the book gives no other exceptions.
