# Calibrate hedges

**Rule:** Use enough [[Hedge]]s to match your actual certainty, and no more. Avoid both mealy-mouthed stacking and flat certainty.
**Why (reader effect):** Hedges signal how well you balance caution and confidence, so they shape how readers judge your character; stacked hedges sound evasive.

## Diagnose
- Count hedges per claim (see [[Patterns - Hedges and intensifiers]]): three or more in one sentence is usually too many.
- Ask: how sure am I? Does the wording match?
- For an unhedged claim ask whether the evidence would support it flatly (it rarely does in academic writing).

## Revise
1. Cut stacked hedges to one (e.g. *suggests that aspects of*).
2. Prefer *suggest* / *indicate* when 100% certainty is lacking but confidence is real.
3. Keep the hedge on the verb or quantity it applies to; do not hedge facts.
4. Use the Goldilocks test: not too much, not too little (see [[Diagnose hedge and intensifier balance]]).

## Examples
> ✗ There seems to be some evidence to suggest that certain differences ... could derive from historical influences possibly traceable to ...
> ✓ This evidence suggests that aspects of Japanese and Western rhetoric differ because of...
> Too certain: I prove that people with a gun in their home use it to kill themselves...
> Just right: My research indicates that people with a gun in their homes are more likely to use it to kill themselves or a family member...

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Even Crick and Watson hedged ("We wish to suggest a structure"); confidence does not require flatness. But stripping hedges is legitimate when real certainty exists.
- Strong writers use first person hedges (*we believe*) sparingly; the book hedges its own claims too.
