# Pose a problem, not just a topic

**Rule:** Plan the piece as posing a problem your readers want solved, not as passing on information that interests you. State that problem in the introduction.
**Why (reader effect):** a topic alone invites "So what?"; readers who don't already care shrug. You get one shot at answering that question.

## Diagnose
- Does the introduction say only what the piece is *about* (topic, background, trend)?
- Could a reader respond "Who cares?" after the last introduction sentence?
- Is there any stated cost, or any unknown that matters?

## Revise
1. Name the condition, then ask "So what?" and state the cost *to your readers*.
2. Frame it with a shared context + *but* (see [[Open with a shared context you can qualify]]).
3. Close with the solution ([[End the introduction with a solution that names key themes]]).

## Examples
> ✗ "...many now 'binge,' downing several alcoholic drinks quickly... It has drawn the attention of parents, administrators, and researchers."
> ✓ "...it has been cited in at least six deaths, many injuries, and considerable destruction of property... we must try to control its worst costs by educating students."

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Readers already deeply engaged and knowledgeable will read through weak introductions (the book calls this a lucky writer's advantage), but do not count on it.
