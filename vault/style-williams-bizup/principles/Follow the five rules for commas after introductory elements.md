# Follow the five rules for commas after introductory elements

**Rule (rules, not choices):**
1. Always put a comma if the reader might misparse the structure.
2. Never end an introductory clause/phrase with a semicolon.
3. No comma right after a subordinating conjunction when the subject follows (*Although, the art...* is wrong).
4. Avoid a comma after a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, yet, for, so, or, nor*) when the subject follows.
5. Comma after a short introductory word/phrase that comments on the whole sentence or links it to the previous (*Fortunately, However, Nevertheless*).
**Why (reader effect):** These mark expected pauses; violating them misleads readers about structure.

## Diagnose
- Find each sentence opener; identify type: subordinate clause, adverb, conjunction.
- Check "conjunction + comma + subject" and "opener + semicolon".

## Revise
1. Apply the five rules above.
2. Do not start many sentences with an element + comma: a series feels hesitant.
3. If a long tangle of openers needs fixing by punctuation, revise the sentence instead.

## Examples
> ✗ When a lawyer concludes her argument has to be easily remembered by a jury.
> ✓ When a lawyer concludes, her argument has to be...
> ✗ Although the Administration knew ... Saudi Arabia; it did not...
> ✓ ... Saudi Arabia, it did not...

## When not to apply / exceptions
- No comma after *now, thus, hence* (*Now it is clear...*).
- With conjunction + interrupter, heavy punctuation (*Yet, during this period, prices...*) is correct but slower; *Yet during this period prices...* also correct (p. 218).
