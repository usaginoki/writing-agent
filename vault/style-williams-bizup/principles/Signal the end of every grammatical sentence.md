# Signal the end of every grammatical sentence

**Rule (hard rule):** Wherever one grammatical sentence (main clause + dependents) stops and the next starts, put a signal: period, semicolon, comma + coordinating conjunction, or one of the rarer marks. Never leave two grammatical sentences with no boundary (fused/run-on).
**Why (reader effect):** Readers must know where a sentence ends; a fused sentence signals that the writer does not know the basics (p. 210). Beyond that, the choice of signal sets the effect.

## Diagnose
- Mark every main clause (subject + finite verb). Check the join between each pair.
- Run-on: nothing at all between them. Comma splice: only a comma ([[Grammar Terms]]).
- Count short sentences in a row (choppy) and long chained ones (sprawl).

## Revise
1. Pick a signal from [[Patterns - Punctuation choices]]; period is the default.
2. If many short sentences result, fold some into subordinate clauses or phrases (one grammatical sentence).
3. If you chain too many, split: see [[Sprawl]].

## Examples
> ✗ ...remedied problems of registration and voting this had political consequences throughout the South.
> ✓ ...registration and voting. This had political consequences... / ...voting; those laws had... / When Congress passed civil rights laws ... in 1967, they had...

## When not to apply / exceptions
- Ten ways exist; 3 conventional, 4 debatable, 3 stylish. Comma alone is condemned by many teachers as a "comma splice" (p. 213): know your reader.
- Punctuated sentence (capital to period) and grammatical sentence differ: same grammar can be one long or seven short punctuated sentences with different feel (p. 209).
