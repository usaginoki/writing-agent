# Lens: Global structure & motivation
**Question this lens asks:** Does the reader see what problem this answers, what the answer is, and how each part (section, paragraph) serves it, from the opening segment alone?
**Level:** document, section, paragraph · **Quick mode:** GM1 (problem/motivation), GM3 (point at end of opening, with themes), GM5 (relevance and order). Run the first 20% of the text only unless asked for more.
**Decisions this lens surfaces to the user (never make silently):**
- Main point/claim: what the document is for. If it cannot be extracted, ask; never invent one.
- Problem framing: practical (reader should do something) vs conceptual (reader should understand something). Changes the whole introduction.
- The cost: who is hurt, or which larger question this answers. Only the author knows the real stakes and audience.
- What to cut: any sentence or section failing the relevance test (may be the author's favorite material or required content).
- Section order: any reorder, esp. chronological/narrative to coordinate/logical.
- Point placement: stated up front (default) vs withheld to the conclusion (needs a promise, GM4).
- Prelude/coda: adding or removing hooks (genre and taste).
- Conclusion content: the "now what?" further question is the author's claim to make.

Triage order: document first (GM1, GM2, GM3, GM4), then sections, then paragraphs (GM5-GM9). Fix beginnings before anything else; weak beginnings make later sentence-level fixes pointless.

## Checks (in order of payoff)

### GM1 · Introduction poses a problem, not a topic
- **Symptom:** run the motivation diagnostic:
  1. Practical or conceptual?
  2. Draw a line after the introduction; if you can't find it fast, readers can't.
  3. Divide into shared context | problem | solution; if you can't, it's unfocused.
  4. First word after the context is but/however?
  5. Split the problem into condition | cost.
  6. Solution underlined at the end?
  - Red flags: intro states only what the piece is *about* (topic, trend, background); the last sentence invites "Who cares?"; the problem is a label (*binge drinking*, *cancer*) = condition with no cost.
- **Fix:**
  1. Name the condition.
  2. Ask "So what?" and answer in 1-3 sentences: that is the cost, in terms *these* readers feel.
  3. Practical: palpable cost (deaths, money, loss) so the reader asks "What do we do?".
  4. Conceptual: state ignorance as a statement ("we do not know why...", not "What causes...?") plus a larger question it would help answer ("if we knew, we might understand...").
  5. Match types: practical condition + practical cost + action; conceptual + conceptual + claim.
  6. If you cannot answer "So what?", surface to the user; the problem may not be worth stating. Do not fabricate costs.
- **✗/✓:** ✗ "...many now 'binge'... It has drawn the attention of parents, administrators, and researchers." ✓ "...it has been cited in at least six deaths, many injuries, and considerable destruction of property... we must try to control its worst costs by educating students."
- **Conceptual trap:** ✗ "What color were Lincoln's socks at Gettysburg?" (no larger question) ✓ "How did Lincoln plan the Address?" (leads to his creative process).
- **Assigned topic:** still needs a problem. Ask the user to complete: "I am writing about... / I care because... / The point I make is..." and name one reader who'd care.
- **Leave it when:** readers are already engaged experts who will read through (do not rely on it; still prefer a cost); notes/memos to a reader who already knows the stakes; the piece is reference material (documentation, spec, FAQ); a one-paragraph text (use GM3 instead).
- **Vault:** [[Pose a problem, not just a topic]] · [[State a problem as condition plus cost]] · [[Find the cost by asking So what]] · [[Choose practical or conceptual framing deliberately]] · [[State practical costs that matter to your readers]] · [[Frame conceptual problems as ignorance plus a larger question]] · [[State a problem even for an assigned topic]] · [[Diagnose whether an introduction motivates]] · [[So what test]]

### GM2 · Shared context + contrast marker
- **Symptom:** introduction opens with topic dump or jumps to the problem with no ground to stand on; shift from "what we know" to "what's wrong" is unmarked (no *but/however*); context is unrelated to the problem.
- **Fix:** 1. Open with 1-2 sentences readers already accept: history, recent event, common belief, or (journals) a literature review. 2. Make it something the problem actually qualifies or overturns. 3. Start the problem sentence with *but / however / on the other hand*. 4. If it still doesn't read as a challenge, tighten the context.
- **✗/✓:** ✗ "...drink and often drink hard. A new kind of drinking known as 'binge' drinking is spreading..." ✓ "...drink and often drink hard. **But** a new kind of drinking known as 'binge' drinking is spreading..."
- **Leave it when:** the piece jumps straight to the problem and the problem is already vivid (some pieces do); the genre has its own opening convention (abstract, memo "Recommendation:" line); notes.
- **Vault:** [[Open with a shared context you can qualify]] · [[Signal the problem with but or however]] · [[Patterns - Introductions]]

### GM3 · Point sentence ends the opening segment and names key themes
- **Symptom:** Where's-the-point test: draw a line after the opening segment of the document, each section, each substantial paragraph; underline the point. Problems: point at the unit's end (reader sees rambling until the last line); opening is background only; no point sentence; point sentence uses generic words ("effectiveness of training") while the body develops other terms ("inconsistent", "no measurable effect"); opening not clearly shorter than the body.
- **Fix:**
  1. Isolate or write a short opening (paragraph: 1-2 sentences; section: a paragraph or more; document: the introduction).
  2. Make the point its last sentence, right before the long body.
  3. Put the 2-4 terms the body develops in the point, late in the sentence (stress).
  4. Cut or merge weak preliminary sentences.
  5. Optionally restate the point at the unit's end as a conclusion.
  6. Practical document: the point is an action ("we must..."); conceptual: a claim ("we found...").
- **✗/✓:** ✗ "Thirty sixth-grade students wrote essays that were analyzed to determine the effectiveness of eight weeks of training..." (point last) ✓ "In this study, thirty sixth-grade students were taught to distinguish fact from opinion. They did so successfully during the instruction period, but the effect was inconsistent and less than predicted, and six months after instruction ended, the instruction had no measurable effect."
- **Sentence level:** ✗ "Resistance in Nevada against its use as a waste disposal site has been heated." ✓ "Nevada has heatedly resisted its use as a waste disposal site." Fix a complex sentence by putting the point clause first.
- **Leave it when:** short transitional or aside paragraphs; a paragraph whose point comes last but whose section point is already clear (see GM8); a deliberate withheld point (GM4); exploratory literary essay for patient readers; fiction.
- **Vault:** [[Open every unit with a short framing segment]] · [[Put the point sentence at the end of the opening segment]] · [[Name key themes toward the end of the point sentence]] · [[End the introduction with a solution that names key themes]] · [[Where's the point test]] · [[Patterns - Framing openings for units]] · [[Don't announce your point; state it]]

### GM4 · Withheld point carries a promise
- **Symptom:** point absent from the introduction; reader reaches the conclusion before learning the claim; introduction ends with nothing signalling what comes.
- **Fix:** default is to move the point into the introduction (telling it won't bore readers motivated by a problem). If the user chooses to withhold: end the introduction with a sentence that promises the claim to come and lists the main themes; deliver the claim in the conclusion using the same terms. Ask the user before choosing withholding.
- **✗/✓:** ✓ "This study examines how X, Y and Z shape...; the conclusion argues..." (schematic).
- **Leave it when:** the user explicitly wants suspense (mystery-style essay, argumentative build, persuasive writing to a hostile reader) and has a good reason; then enforce only the promise + themes.
- **Vault:** [[Promise a withheld point at the end of the introduction]] · [[End the introduction with a solution that names key themes]] · [[Write conclusions by reversing the introduction]]

### GM5 · Every sentence relevant to the unit's point
- **Symptom:** relevance test: state the point; classify each sentence as background/context, point, reason, evidence/data, explanation of reasoning or method, or other views. Sentences fitting none are flagged (e.g., spelling errors, essay length in a fact/opinion study).
- **Fix:** 1. Delete flagged sentences, or 2. link them explicitly to the point, or 3. move them to where they serve. Never delete silently: list cuts for the user (they may be required content or loved material).
- **✗/✓:** ✗ in the fact/opinion study: "These essays were also badly organized in several ways." "They also produced fewer spelling and punctuation errors." ✓ remove; keep only material on distinguishing fact from opinion.
- **Leave it when:** transitions and asides that orient the reader; texts that are deliberately inclusive (reference, archive, lab notebook, meeting notes); anecdote that serves a stated theme; required boilerplate.
- **Vault:** [[Make every sentence relevant to the point]] · [[Relevance test]]

### GM6 · Key themes recur in the body in the same words
- **Symptom:** key-term recurrence test: bold key terms in the point sentence; mark exact recurrences in the body; then list recurrent body terms and check they appear in the point. Problems: opening terms never return; important body terms never announced; synonyms rotate for the same concept ("elegant variation").
- **Fix:**
  1. Reuse the exact key terms, in topic/stress positions.
  2. Replace synonym rotation with the announced term.
  3. If a body term matters but is unannounced, add it to the point sentence.
  4. Keep subjects consistent and old-before-new (hand off to the cohesion lens).
- **✗/✓:** ✓ Alzheimer's model: point names *early and accurate diagnosis*, *informing*, *human problem*; body repeats *diagnoses, accuracy, predict, early diagnosis, human tragedy*.
- **Leave it when:** technical terms with true synonyms in the field are interchangeable and the reader knows it (rare; ask); poetry or literary prose where variation is the point; very short text.
- **Vault:** [[Repeat key themes in the body]] · [[Key-term recurrence test]] · [[Name key themes toward the end of the point sentence]]

### GM7 · Order is visible, signalled, and not a narrative of your thinking
- **Symptom:** index-card test: write each paragraph's point on a card, shuffle, have the reader (or you, cold) reorder; mismatch means invisible order. Also: you cannot name the ordering principle (chronological, coordinate, logical); no signal words; draft reads "first I... then I found..."; the main point arrives last because the structure follows discovery.
- **Fix:**
  1. Name the principle per section.
  2. Chronological: earlier to later, cause to effect (*first, then, finally; as a result*).
  3. Coordinate: parallel pillars ordered by importance or complexity (*first, second; also; more important*).
  4. Logical: example/generalization, premise/conclusion, assertion/contradiction (*for example, on the other hand, it follows that*).
  5. Convert narrative drafts to coordinate or logical: reorder, add signals, rewrite openings; chronological evidence may stay as support after the point-first opening.
  6. Reordering is a user decision: propose it, show the old and new outline.
- **✗/✓:** ✗ "Thirty students wrote essays... In an essay before... after four weeks... in a final essay..." (point last) ✓ point first ("They did so successfully..., but... no measurable effect"), then the chronology as support.
- **Leave it when:** content is truly a sequence (procedure, history, incident report, changelog); exploratory essays and journals that show thinking in progress for patient readers; cause-and-effect narratives.
- **Vault:** [[Order parts chronologically, coordinately, or logically and signal it]] · [[Do not narrate your thinking]] · [[Index-card order test]] · [[Preview a difficult document by its points]]

### GM8 · Boundaries visible; weak paragraphs rescued by strong section openers
- **Symptom:** cannot draw a line after the introduction or after a section's opening; headings are generic labels ("Results", "Discussion") with no themes; long paragraphs begin with detail and put the point last while the section gives no point either.
- **Fix:** 1. Fix document and section introductions first. 2. Make headings carry the section's key themes (drop headings in the final form only if the field doesn't use them, keep the structure). 3. Then give long paragraphs an opening frame; move the point to the opening segment's end when cheap. 4. Preview test on your own draft: highlight the question, main claim, each section's intro, point and themes; if you can't, readers can't.
- **✗/✓:** ✗ paragraph opens with fossil sequences, point ("By mapping every fossil... recreate how and when animals evolved") last. ✓ opens with that point, then the details.
- **Leave it when:** a section point is clear and a paragraph is imperfect (spend no effort); short paragraphs; fields or genres with fixed headings (IMRaD, memo headers): keep the headings and put themes in the section's first sentence instead.
- **Vault:** [[Mark where introductions and sections begin and end]] · [[Introduce sections clearly so imperfect paragraphs survive]] · [[Spend revision effort on beginnings]] · [[Preview a difficult document by its points]]

### GM9 · Conclusion reverses the introduction
- **Symptom:** the document ends by trailing off, a new topic, a bare summary, or a repeated list; no statement of significance; no further question.
- **Fix:** when nothing better suggests itself: (1) restate the main claim in a sentence; (2) significance: new "So what?" or the cost recast as benefit ("If we do not control bingeing soon, many more students will die." / "If we start now, we will save many lives."); (3) a further unknown or problem: "Now what?"; (4) optional coda echoing the prelude (popular writing only). The further question (3) is a claim: confirm with the user.
- **✗/✓:** ✓ "Though we can come at the problem of bingeing from several directions, the most important is education... But that means each university must devote time and resources to it."
- **Leave it when:** conclusions vary more than introductions, and a better ending exists (call to action, an image, a result table); short documents and notes need no conclusion; emails and memos end with the ask or next step; scientific genres rarely use codas.
- **Vault:** [[Write conclusions by reversing the introduction]] · [[Patterns - Conclusions]] · [[Use a prelude only when the genre allows]] · [[Patterns - Preludes and codas]]

### GM10 · Prelude is optional and never replaces the problem
- **Symptom:** an anecdote, quotation or startling fact opens the piece but the problem statement never arrives; or a gimmick unrelated to the key themes; or a prelude in a scientific paper.
- **Fix:** keep only a prelude tied to the problem's themes (quotation, fact, anecdote, or a combination), then shared context, then *but* + problem, then solution. Remove or flag a prelude that substitutes for the problem. Adding a prelude is a user decision.
- **✗/✓:** ✓ "'If you're old enough to fight for your country, you're old enough to drink to it.'... Jim S., president of Omega Alpha, no longer has a chance to do either." then "Drinking has been part of American college life... But..."
- **Leave it when:** genre is humanities or general-audience (a prelude is fine, leave it if it feeds the themes); natural and social science (don't add one).
- **Vault:** [[Use a prelude only when the genre allows]] · [[Patterns - Preludes and codas]]

### GM11 · Formula resisted out of fear of boredom
- **Symptom:** the author (or editor) is tempted to skip the point-first, theme-naming pattern because it feels cut and dried, or wants "variety" in structure.
- **Fix:** apply the patterns by default; most readers read to understand, have little time, and notice substance, not formula. Only the writer is conscious of the formula.
- **Leave it when:** exploratory literary essay for patient readers who want to follow the thinking; then tell the user what is traded away (reader effort) and leave it.
- **Vault:** [[Accept formulaic structure for clarity]]

## Run order (whole-document pass)
1. Extract the skeleton: question/problem, main claim, each section's heading + first sentence, each paragraph's first and last sentence.
2. Run GM1-GM4 on the introduction; stop and ask the user the decisions above if the claim or the cost cannot be extracted.
3. Run GM7-GM8 on the skeleton (order, boundaries) before touching paragraphs.
4. Run GM3, GM5, GM6 section by section, then on paragraphs longer than ~5 sentences.
5. Run GM9-GM10 on the ending and any prelude.
6. Report as: per-check finding, proposed fix, and a separate "needs your decision" list. Apply only the fixes that do not change claim, order, or content.
7. Hand off sentence-level issues (old/new, topic strings, stress, characters/actions, concision) to the other lenses; do not fix them here.

## Templates
**Practical introduction** (reader should act)
1. Shared context: what the reader knows or accepts (1-2 sentences).
2. *But/However* + condition (what is happening).
3. Cost: palpable consequences for this reader (answer to "So what?").
4. Solution: the action, with key themes at the end ("we must..., by ...").
Goal: reader asks "What do we do?", not "So what?".

**Conceptual introduction** (reader should understand)
1. Shared context: what we know (or the literature).
2. *But we don't know...* (small question stated as ignorance).
3. Larger question: "If we knew, we might understand..." (why it matters).
4. Solution: the claim or finding, with key themes ("In this study we found...").

**Optional prelude** (before step 1): quotation, startling fact or short anecdote tied to the themes; popular and humanities writing only.

**Conclusion (reversed)**: restate the claim > significance (new "So what?" or cost as benefit) > "Now what?" (further problem/question) > optional coda echoing the prelude.

**Issue -> point -> discussion unit** (paragraph, section, or document)
- Issue: 1-2 sentences framing the unit (paragraph), a paragraph or more (section), the introduction (document); always much shorter than the body.
- Point: last sentence of the issue; names 2-4 key themes late in the sentence.
- Discussion: longer body; consistent topics, same key terms repeated, old-before-new, every sentence a reason, evidence, method, other view or context; order named and signalled; optionally restate the point at the end.
- Compact model: "Researchers have made strides in the early and accurate diagnosis of Alzheimer's, but those diagnoses have raised a new human problem about informing those at risk..." then the body repeats *diagnosis, accuracy, predict, human tragedy*.

**Withheld point**: "... This paper examines X, Y and Z; the conclusion argues that [claim promised, not stated]."

## Genre adjustments
- **Research paper:** problem is usually conceptual (small question + larger question); a literature review is the shared context; solution states the finding in the introduction's last sentence; no prelude; headings exist (put themes in them; IMRaD sections still need a framing first sentence). Conclusion: restate, significance, further research.
- **Essay (humanities / general):** prelude and coda acceptable; practical framing common in general-audience essays. Exploratory personal essays may withhold or wander; surface it as a choice and promise at least the themes.
- **Notes / personal notes:** mostly leave alone; apply only GM3 to any note meant for later re-reading (one-line point at the top) and GM5 only if asked. No problem statement, no conclusion.
- **Memo / email / report:** practical problem; lead with the ask or recommendation (point first), then the cost and the detail; cost must matter to this recipient; no prelude, no coda; headings carry themes; the order is usually logical or coordinate.
- **Documentation / reference:** leave structure alone except section openers that state what the section covers and when to use it.
- **Fiction / poetry:** out of scope.

## Over-correction traps
- Inventing a problem or cost the author doesn't have. Ask; never fabricate stakes, and never inflate costs (alarmism).
- Forcing the shared-context / *but* formula onto every document, including notes, memos and short pieces.
- Hard-coding "point last in opening" onto every short paragraph, transition or aside.
- Replacing a plain "This paper..." sentence with an announcement; also reversing it: stating a point is not "announcing" it, but "In this section I will discuss X" is metadiscourse that names a topic, not a point. Prefer the real claim.
- Deleting "irrelevant" material that serves the user's purpose (reference, evidence, deliberate context); present a cut list instead.
- Reordering a sequence that is legitimately chronological (procedures, incident timelines).
- Forcing identical repeated terms where true synonyms are conventional or ambiguity would arise; or repeating a term so often it becomes mechanical.
- Pasting a prelude or coda into scientific or technical writing.
- Rewriting the author's claim while "stating the point": a point sentence must say what the author says, nothing stronger.
- Moving a withheld point forward without asking when suspense is the author's deliberate choice.
- Spending effort on sentence-level polish before document-level structure is settled.
